## 1. The bullet-hell inside Undertale

### How the combat actually works
Undertale's encounters run on a four-button menu — FIGHT, ACT, ITEM, MERCY — but the *defense* phase is a literal danmaku minigame: your SOUL (a red heart) sits inside the "bullet board" and you steer it with arrow keys to dodge each monster's bullet pattern. The board's dimensions and the SOUL's physics change per attack and per "SOUL mode" (e.g., blue SOUL = gravity platformer physics, green SOUL = locked in place with a directional shield against Undyne's arrows). ACT is the pacifist verb set — flirt, console, pet, flex — that changes a monster's mood until MERCY→Spare resolves the fight without killing. [snippet only — Undertale Fandom wiki "Encounter"/"Bullet Board"; mechanics are uncontroversial]

Parry Everything's design essay states the inversion cleanly: in Undertale, shmup mechanics are a *defense* system — "the player must move around a little icon representing themselves… so it doesn't collide with any objects on screen" — which "repositions dodging as engaging gameplay, making passivity more compelling than aggression," and "success in combat in Undertale is much more contingent on one's ability to navigate enemy attacks, which take the form of 'bullet' patterns in a shoot em' up or shmup styled dodging sequence." [fetched & verified]

### How hard it gets
Wikipedia (citing reviews): the game was "designed to be 'easy and engaging'; difficulty was optional." [fetched & verified] The genocide route is where the danmaku teeth come out: Undyne the Undying (green-SOUL shield mode plus relentless spear volleys) and Sans, "considered one of the hardest video game bosses ever," with blue-SOUL platforming physics, attacks that hit during the *menu*, and a final attack that is simply waiting. [snippet only — GameRant/Eneba] Critical Video Game Studies' case study frames the difficulty as narrative: on genocide, "The game actively wants you to stop playing this route… Undertale does it with difficulty"; Sans "cheats by attacking you during the menu options" and "his final move? Waiting for the player to quit out of boredom." Meanwhile pacifist inverts stakes at the other end: "it is impossible to die in the final boss fight of pacifist because the determination you've built to this point literally won't let you." [fetched & verified] That's the full span: the default path can't be failed at the climax; the opt-in path is genre-hard.

### The Touhou debt (developer statements)
- Toby Fox tweet, May 2018: "I've been a fan of Touhou since I was 10…" [snippet only — x.com/tobyfox/status/999210405959426048; both archive backends failed on the full text]
- The 2018 Toby Fox × ZUN interview (Den Famicogamer, via clarste's widely-cited translation summary, retrieved from Wayback): Toby's been a Touhou fan "since middle school, when he downloaded the demos of PCB and EoSD"; his favorite single danmaku is Youmu's PCB stage-6 midboss spellcard, because it "looks like it will slow down and then it doesn't — playing with the player's expectations like that really stood out to him." Both men compose the music *before* building the game around it. And ZUN's retention principle, which Toby endorsed: "the music needs to be good… to counteract the frustration of losing. Without a good feeling to cancel out the bad feeling, the player might just quit. The harder the fight, the better the music needs to be." [fetched & verified — note this is a fan translation/summary, not an official transcript]
- Nintendo interview (via Nintendo Everything): influenced "by every game I like – for example, EarthBound, Shin Megami Tensei, Mario & Luigi, Touhou Project, etc."; "The ACT and MERCY commands were inspired by Shin Megami Tensei. I played just a bit of that game and loved talking to the monsters in it"; Metal Gear Solid taught him "You don't have to follow the rules… it's more interesting when you don't." [fetched & verified]
- Wikipedia's influence list adds Mario & Luigi (defensive action segments), Moon: Remix RPG Adventure (non-violence), Cave Story, and the Mother series (aesthetic, unsettling-under-cute atmosphere; Fox had made EarthBound ROM hacks, where Megalovania originated). [fetched & verified]

### Patterns as characterization — and where that idea comes from
This is the deepest link to Touhou, and ZUN has stated the design theory explicitly (ZUN × Hiroyuki interview, Touhou Garakuta): "One big shortcoming in shoot em' ups is that enemy attacks often lack a distinctive identity. I tried my hand in naming these attack patterns." Named, they "gained meaning" and "became more interesting" — but "Without a story behind it, the names meant nothing… if you want to make the game's bullet patterns interesting, one factor is including a story," and "If I want to make players interested in them, I need to make them care about the characters who are using these patterns." [fetched & verified] That is the entire Undertale combat thesis, stated by ZUN about spell cards.

Undertale executes it per-monster: Parry Everything documents that "the attack conveys the emotional state of the monster which used it" — Shyren "loves to sing but is too timid, needing gentle encouragement from the player humming along"; Woshua is "obsessed with washing things"; Aaron the flexing seahorse "will become so swept up in out-flexing you… that he'll flex himself right out of combat." [fetched & verified] Fan-lore framing: bullets are a monster's magic, "a reflection of themselves" — Papyrus's pattern literally spells "cool dude" with a bone on a skateboard. [snippet only] Attacks are also mood-state machines: ACTs visibly change what gets thrown at you (flirt with Aaron and the flexes escalate), so the bullet board doubles as the conversation's body language.

### Onboarding people who'd never dodged a bullet
The verified pieces of the on-ramp: HP as a large buffer (a graze that would kill in Touhou costs a sliver of HP); cheap ubiquitous healing items; deliberately easy default balance ("easy and engaging" by design); patterns built from "the barest, simplest forms of spacial and temporal awareness" that escalate gradually (Parry Everything [fetched & verified]); jokes *as* patterns (Papyrus, the dog attacks, bullet-pattern birthday cards in the game's own text [snippet only]); and every pattern arriving pre-framed by dialogue, music, and a character you're curious about — which is ZUN's spell-card theory applied at tutorial intensity. The stakes inversion matters too: dying in Undertale mid-run costs a little progress and often yields new dialogue; genre-difficulty exists but is quarantined behind an explicit moral opt-in.

## 2. Why modern audiences loved it

### Numbers
- Steam sales: 530,343 copies by end of 2015; 1M by Feb 2016; ~3.5M Steam players by July 2018; SteamSpy band 5–10M Steam owners. [fetched & verified — Wikipedia] Aggregators put Steam units around 6.3M. [fetched — levvvel, secondary source]
- Steam reviews: "Overwhelmingly Positive," 96%. Count conflicts: my Steam store fetch surfaced 155,979; levvvel says 203,000+; recent press snippets say ~240K. (See Contradictions.) [fetched, count uncertain]
- Metacritic 92 (PC/PS4), 93 (Switch); OpenCritic 97% recommend. [fetched & verified]
- Price: $9.99 at launch and today. [fetched & verified — Steam store]

### What carried it (critics' consensus)
Per Wikipedia's reception synthesis: the writing ("consistently funny" — IGN; best-written game of 2015 — The Escapist), the characters ("individuality and emotional depth"), the music (leitmotif structure; Fox composed everything in FL Studio, >90% written for the game), the mercy premise, and the combat system as *novelty* ("incredibly nuanced" — Game Informer). The one consistently criticized element was the art. [fetched & verified] NPR: the game "shaped the entertainment tastes of an entire generation." [fetched & verified via Wikipedia] The fandom's scale shows in second-order artifacts: Megalovania's escape into general meme culture, Sans as a Smash Ultimate Mii costume, and a fan-game ecosystem robust enough that Undertale Yellow shipped after seven years of development. [fetched & verified]

### RPG-audience vs shmup-audience composition
No direct survey data found — this is **thin evidence** territory. The best indirect measure: Undertale has ~150–240K Steam reviews, while ZeroRanger — arguably the most acclaimed pure indie shmup of its era, also Overwhelmingly Positive — has **1,634**. [both fetched & verified] Whatever the exact ratio, roughly two orders of magnitude more people happily completed Undertale's bullet patterns than buy excellent pure shmups. The audience was overwhelmingly *not* genre-native; the danmaku came to them disguised.

A useful dissent: Uninterpretative's "Distrusting Undertale" — written by a shmup player — calls the combat "good design, poorly executed" (mushy SOUL hitbox, aesthetic constraints imposing "a hard upper limit on the possibility of difficulty") and found the segments "completely unengaging." [fetched & verified] The mass audience did not care. Mechanical depth was not what they were buying.

## 3. Toby Fox's development and business model

- **Solo dev**: programming, writing, design, and all music by Fox (art help from Temmie Chang), in GameMaker Studio, over 32 months. [fetched & verified — Wikipedia]
- **Kickstarter, June 24 2013**: goal **$5,000**, raised **$51,124** from 2,398 backers — 10x goal. [fetched & verified — Wikipedia]
- **The free demo came first**: released May 23, 2013, about a month *before* the Kickstarter, representing ~25% of the eventual game. The pitch was playable before it asked for money. [fetched & verified — Wikipedia]
- **Launch**: Sept 15, 2015, $9.99. [fetched & verified]
- **Music as spread vector**: the OST (self-composed, leitmotif-dense) became the fandom's lingua franca; Megalovania predates the game (Homestuck, EarthBound ROM hack) and outgrew it. ZUN's stated principle — good music cancels the frustration of losing — is the design logic; the virality was the side effect. [fetched & verified]

### Deltarune's free-first model
- **Chapter 1, Oct 31 2018: released free**, as a surprise, disguised as a "survey program." Fox said remaining chapters were "intended to be released after the completion of the entire game" and that he "needed to form a team, claiming it would be impossible to finish the game alone" — the free chapter was explicitly an audience-and-team-building event, not a product. [fetched & verified — Wikipedia]
- **Chapter 2, Sept 17 2021: also free**; Fox's stated reason was that the COVID-19 pandemic had "made the world really tough for everybody recently." [fetched & verified — Wikipedia]
- **Audience built**: after Chapter 2, the *free demo* hit "around 100,000 concurrent players on Steam, far higher than Undertale's lifetime record." [fetched & verified — Wikipedia]
- **Conversion, June 5 2025**: Chapters 1–4 as a paid package, **$24.99**, "You only have to buy the game once - upcoming Chapters will be added as free updates." Fox's pricing ethic, verbatim: **"I only feel right charging for what there currently is, not what I say there will be. Therefore, this $24.99 to me represents the exact price of 4 Chapters!"** and "it's my hope that as we complete more Chapters, you'll feel like this game was a super super super super good deal." [fetched & verified — toby.fangamer.com newsletter]
- **Outcome**: among the highest-grossing games on Steam at launch; best-selling digital game on Switch 2 at that console's launch; 100% OpenCritic recommend; the free Chapter 5 update (June 2026) drew 290K+ Steam concurrents. [fetched & verified — Wikipedia]
- The oft-quoted line that his original plan was to ship Chapters 3–5 together but he "didn't think anybody really wanted to wait that long": [snippet only — reported via GameRant/ComicBook paraphrases of a Fox newsletter; I could not locate the primary text].
- **No Patreon found** for Fox — funding appears to be demo-first crowdfunding, then Undertale revenue funding a hired Deltarune team (producer Robert Sephazon, programmers). [fetched — absence of evidence in Wikipedia/newsletters]

## 4. The free-first / demo-first path for indie arcade games

**Cave Story (2004)** — five years of solo nights-and-weekends work by Pixel, released as freeware Dec 20, 2004; "slowly gained popularity on the Internet" via community translation; became the archetypal indie game. Paid ports began *six years later* (WiiWare 2010, Cave Story+ Steam 2011, 3DS, Switch); Cave Story+ alone sold ~590,104 Steam units by July 2018 — while the freeware original stayed legally free the whole time. Free distribution and paid conversion coexisted for a decade+. [fetched & verified — Wikipedia]

**Touhou** — one man since 1995 doing "programming, graphics, writing, and music"; games sold at Comiket first (EoSD sold poorly "for one day at a single Comiket booth" before mail-order cult growth), with **free trial versions "typically distributed prior to release."** The real engine is the near-free fan license: minimal restrictions on derivative works (attribution, no large-commercial distribution without permission — and, notably, fan creators asked to "refrain from crowdfunding"), producing 2,372 dedicated circles at Comiket 77 and an endless derivative-game ecosystem that later flowed onto PS4/Switch/Steam via Play,Doujin!. ZUN "did not want the Touhou Project series to be officially commercialized." The fandom *is* the marketing department. [fetched & verified — Wikipedia Touhou Project, ANN]

**ZeroRanger** — began ~2008 as freeware **FINALBOSS**, with free releases across a decade (FINALBOSS -Raw Label-, Oct 2015, still archived on the official site); renamed for the 2018 commercial release; System Erasure's own site: "ZeroRanger started development as a simple shoot'em up somewhere around 2008 originally under the name of FINALBOSS." Result: $11.99, Overwhelmingly Positive — and **1,634 reviews**. [fetched & verified — se-made.com, ZeroRanger wiki via Wayback, Steam] A decade of freeware built a devoted cult and genre-canon status, *not* a mass audience. This is the ceiling of free-first without a non-genre hook.

**Crimzon Clover** — Yotsubane (CAVE superplayer Clover-TAC), five years solo, **free trial versions during development** plus a paid trial at Comiket 78, full release at Comiket 79 (2011), Steam WORLD IGNITION 2014 (Destructoid 8.5, GameRankings 82.5%). No public sales figures. [fetched & verified — Wikipedia] Same shape as ZeroRanger: trial-first works as a doujin ritual; outcomes stay genre-sized.

**Vampire Survivors** — the *cheap wedge* variant: itch.io first, then Steam early access at **$2.99** (Dec 2021), ~£1,100 total asset spend, discovery driven by YouTubers covering it organically (30K concurrents by late Jan 2022, 70K+ in Feb); price raised to $4.99 before 1.0 with no backlash; Galante's wealth estimated at £40M by The Sunday Times (Aug 2024). [fetched & verified — Wikipedia] Lesson: near-free plus streamable spectacle converts at genuinely mass scale; the low price was explicitly an audience-reach decision.

**Patreon as the funding leg** — Game Developer's analysis [fetched & verified]: it works for devs with "pre-existing communities, strong news pipelines, or unique release models (frequent updates, early access)." Real numbers: Paralives ~$41.5K/month (8,511 patrons — a Sims-shaped mass-audience pitch), Sokpop ~$5.9K/month (shipping a game *every month*), Bay 12/Dwarf Fortress ~$8.2K/month after decades of freeware goodwill. Risks: retention demands constant exclusive content, income is volatile, and community management competes with dev time. **I found no example of a shmup dev funded meaningfully via Patreon** [thin evidence — search budget exhausted before this could be run down; treat as "no known precedent" rather than "impossible"].

**Devaluation question**: across every precedent fetched, free/demo-first did not suppress later paid conversion — Cave Story's freeware never blocked 590K paid Steam units; Deltarune's two free chapters produced a chart-topping $24.99 conversion; Undertale's free demo produced a 10x Kickstarter; Touhou's free trials and free fan-license produced the largest doujin franchise in existence. The failure mode in the data is not devaluation, it's *invisibility*: FINALBOSS was free for ten years and stayed niche because the thing being given away only appealed to people who already liked shmups.

## 5. The "trick," generalized

I found no GDC talk on this specific thesis (Fox has never given one), so the analysis base is essays plus ZUN's own statements. The mechanism, assembled from verified sources:

1. **Reframe the verb.** Dodging is *defense inside a conversation*, not a genre exercise (Parry Everything). The player thinks they're listening to a character; the hands are doing Touhou stage-1.
2. **Name the pattern, story the pattern.** ZUN, verbatim: attacks "lack a distinctive identity" until named, and names mean nothing "without a story behind it… I need to make them care about the characters who are using these patterns." Undertale simply pushed the slider further: the character *is* the encounter.
3. **Invert the stakes.** HP buffer + cheap healing + un-failable pacifist climax; genre difficulty exists but as an opt-in narrative punishment (CVGS: difficulty as storytelling — genocide's difficulty is the game telling you to stop).
4. **Music cancels frustration** (ZUN's stated law; Fox's practice). The retry loop is bearable because the loop sounds great.
5. **Mechanical depth is optional.** A shmup critic found the dodging shallow and mushy (Uninterpretative) and it made no commercial difference — because depth wasn't the product; *meaning* was.

**Other games that carried arcade mechanics on story/character/music framing** (all [fetched & verified] via Wikipedia unless noted):
- **NieR: Automata** — PlatinumGames wove top-down/side-scrolling shoot-'em-up sequences and twin-stick hacking minigames through an action RPG; critics praised narrative and score above combat ("lacked depth compared to previous PlatinumGames titles"); Easy-mode auto-chips "automate functions such as attacking, dodging and pod fire" so story players could pass the arcade material. 10M+ copies by Feb 2026. Bullet-hell segments reached ten million people because they were carried by 2B and a story, with an explicit skill bypass.
- **Just Shapes & Beats** — "music-based bullet hell," no shooting at all, 3 hits + checkpoints (6 in bosses), co-op revives, a minimal wordless villain story; Metacritic 83 (Switch), IGF Excellence in Visual Art, Golden Joystick nominations. Pure dodge-game made broadly palatable by music + forgiveness + a grinning antagonist.
- **Everhood** — explicitly the Undertale playbook (rhythm/lane bullet-dodging inside a narrative RPG by a two-person team); Metacritic 81–86; critics called it the "anti-Undertale" and noted the shared "innocence that peeled away." The framing is now a repeatable indie genre.
- **Furi** — boss-rush hybrid of shmup and slash with a synthwave soundtrack "designed… specifically for… the boss/area," each artist chosen to musically represent a boss; Metacritic 74–77 but Steam users "far more positive." Note: its famous PS Plus July 2016 giveaway is **not** confirmed in my fetched source — see Contradictions.

## Synthesis for SPEEDHELL: a free-release playbook

**The core finding: the free release is the distribution mechanism, but the story-game liberties are the payload.** Every free-first *pure* shmup in the record (FINALBOSS/ZeroRanger, Crimzon Clover trials, Touhou trials) converted into a beloved *genre-sized* success — four-digit review counts. Every free-first release that wrapped arcade mechanics in character/music/mystery (Undertale demo, Deltarune Ch1) converted into a *mass* success — six-digit counts. Free-first never devalued anyone; it only amplified whatever hook already existed. So the question isn't "free or not" — it's "what will 100,000 non-shmup players think they're playing?"

**Highest audience-per-unit-of-work liberties for a one-stage arcade shmup**, in order:

1. **Named attacks.** ZUN's spell-card insight is almost free to implement: a name card on screen when the midboss/boss pattern starts. SPEEDHELL already treats patterns as designed set pieces (arrival bloom, speed-gated cancel); naming them converts mechanics the player can't yet read into *events they can talk about*. Cost: strings. This is the single cheapest ZUN/Toby trick.
2. **A boss with a mouth.** One character who talks *during* the fight — taunts tied to player behavior (camping, speed, deaths) — is the Undertale/Furi move. SPEEDHELL already has a camp governor and a referee watching player behavior; wiring those signals to barks makes the game feel like it *sees you*, which is exactly what non-genre players quote in clips. Cost: a text system + writing, no new mechanics.
3. **Music-forward identity.** Sound has landed; treat the OST as a distribution channel (uploadable, named tracks, boss theme entering at a dramatic beat — already the architecture per your audio design). ZUN's law applies directly to a retry-heavy score game: the harder the loop, the better it must sound.
4. **Stakes inversion for the default mode.** An HP-buffer / generous mode as default, with the current tuning as the named "real" mode — Undertale quarantined genre difficulty behind an opt-in and lost nothing; NieR shipped auto-dodge and sold 10M. For a score-driven game, the honest version is: everyone finishes the stage; *score* is where mastery lives. That's also arcade-authentic.
5. **One joke pattern.** A single absurd, screenshot-able attack (Papyrus's "cool dude") does more social-media work than a balance pass.

What *not* to bother with: a full ACT/MERCY narrative system, branching routes, or RPG scaffolding — those were months of Toby's work and your one-stage scope doesn't need them. The verified mechanism is characterization density per pattern, not systems.

**The free→Patreon→full path, given the precedents:**
- **Free release = the Kickstarter demo, not the product.** Undertale's sequence is the model: free playable thing (May 2013) → funding ask a month later (10x'd) → paid full game two years later at $9.99. Deltarune modernized it: free Chapter 1 as a surprise *event* → team-building → paid conversion of a superset ("I only feel right charging for what there currently is"). For SPEEDHELL: ship the one-stage game free as SPEEDHELL (or a subtitled "first loop"), loudly framed as chapter one of something, and let the paid release be a superset that includes it — the Deltarune structure means early adopters are never punished.
- **Patreon expectations, calibrated:** no shmup Patreon precedent was found; realistic comparables (Sokpop, Bay 12) are $5–8K/month *with* relentless output or decades of goodwill, and Game Developer's analysis says Patreon follows a pre-existing community — it doesn't create one. Treat Patreon as the *retention* layer after the free release makes noise (devlogs, pattern-design posts, OST early access), not as primary funding. If the free release lands, a fixed-goal ask (Kickstarter-style, like Toby's) converts a spike better than a subscription converts a trickle. Note ZUN's fan-license quirk: he *bans* crowdfunding for Touhou fan works — the doujin path and the crowdfunding path are alternatives, not a stack.
- **Consider the Vampire Survivors variant** if free feels wrong: $2.99 is functionally free for audience-reach purposes, filters zero buyers, raised later without backlash, and keeps you on Steam's paid infrastructure (reviews, visibility rounds) from day one.
- **Sequencing:** free itch.io build first (ZeroRanger/VS pattern: itch is where freeware cults form and streamers browse), Steam page up simultaneously wishlisting the paid superset.

**Risks, from the record:**
- **Invisibility, not devaluation.** FINALBOSS's decade proves free-plus-excellent-shmup ≠ audience. If SPEEDHELL ships free *without* the character/naming/music layer, the precedent says you get 1,000 devoted players, not 100,000.
- **The genre ceiling is real even at the top**: ZeroRanger, Overwhelmingly Positive, 1,634 reviews. Plan around that number as the no-story baseline.
- **Patreon costs dev time** (exclusive-content treadmill, per Gariépy) — dangerous for a solo dev whose whole edge is shipping.
- **Undertale's shmup was mechanically criticized and won anyway** — the inverse risk: leaning on mechanical depth (SPEEDHELL's actual strength) as the *pitch* selects for the small audience. Depth should be the retention floor under a character-shaped pitch, not the headline.
- One Toby-specific caveat: Deltarune Ch1's free release worked partly because *Undertale's author* released it. A first free release from an unknown buys attention with its hook alone — which is the strongest argument for spending the (small) effort on liberties 1–3 above before shipping.
