# Getting feelings out of a playtester: interview practice, Nielsen, and a pause-and-talk mode for SPEEDHELL

*Research 2026-08-29. 23 searches, ~40 fetched pages; [F] = quote verified in the fetched page, [S] = snippet only. Purpose: Jacob (solo dev, not an expert shmup player) wants to pause at a moment that "feels incoherent" and talk it through with the AI design partner instead of playing a whole run and reconstructing it afterwards.*

## Executive summary

1. **Nielsen's two rules point straight at pause-and-talk.** Think-aloud is "the single most valuable usability engineering method" [1], and the "first rule of usability" is "Watch what people actually do. Do not believe what people say they do" [4] — but he does *not* ban asking about feelings; he bans asking before or long after use, because interviews make people "either remember past use or speculate on future use… extraordinarily weak and often misleading" [5]. An interview paused *at the moment* removes both failures.
2. **Games research already built this.** John Hopson (Bungie, Halo/Destiny) tested mid-playtest feedback for 16 years: the Halo debug menu that "automatically paused the game when it appeared, giving the player space to choose a response" worked; unpaused controller gestures were "forgotten" or misfired; a visible button on the desk was "a permanent reminder that we expected them to push the buttons" [43]. Gow et al. (2010) ran video-cued post-game commentaries with a 20-word feeling list and two fallback prompts — **"What were you doing here? What did you feel here?"** — and found "Frustrated" and "Challenged" the most-used words; their open coding produced seven dimensions: Hard/Easy, In-Control/Controlled, Interested/Bored, Understand/Confused, Satisfied/Dissatisfied, Confident/Cautious, Purposeful/Aimless [22].
3. **Vocabulary scaffolds work, as conversation starters, not metrics.** Microsoft's product reaction cards "introduce a controlled vocabulary for participants" [20]; the pick-5 then interview-on-the-picks step is the point [21]; "There is no evidence that the Desirability Toolkit actually measures desirability" [21].
4. **How to ask:** open questions first, never "why" (feels accusatory — "What happened?" instead), never name a system (leading), never ask the player to design ("Users are not designers" [5]; asking for solutions "wastes research time" [30]), count to seven before filling silence [9], echo the player's own words [7][30], ask the expectation gap: "How did you expect this to work? What did you try?" [30].
5. **Timing:** keep sessions short and non-repetitive — "the player is more likely to confuse two similar episodes" [22], which describes a looping shmup exactly; debrief immediately (Nielsen's "15-minute memory dump while details remain fresh" [3]).
6. **Self-testing caveat:** the developer is "the worst person to playtest your game" for *clarity* (curse of knowledge) [47][48][49], but game feel is first-person by definition (Swink) — Jacob's own flags are valid data for feel and pacing, suspect for readability. For "what killed me?" flags, get a second player; Nielsen's 5-user rule says small tests, many times [6].

## 1. Nielsen / NN/g

- Think-aloud: "you ask test participants to use the system while continuously thinking out loud" [1]. Its weakness: "Filtered statements (vs. brain dump). Users are supposed to say things as soon as they come to mind rather than reflect on their experience." [1] "Typically, you have to prompt users to keep them talking." [1] Neutral prompts: "What's going through your mind?", "Tell me what you're seeing.", "Can you describe your current goal?"; if they ask for help: "What would you try if you were alone?" [3]
- Don't listen to users: "Self-reported data is typically 3 steps removed from the truth" [4]; ask about preferences "Only after they have used a design and have a real feeling for how well it supports them." [4]
- Interviewing: "If you ask leading questions or press respondents for answers, they might make up opinions that don't reflect their real preferences." [5] Anchor to episodes: "Tell me about the last time you [did something]" [17]; "if you ask people how they usually perform a task, they'll often describe an idealized workflow." [5]
- Probing: the Echo ("repeats the last phrase or word the user said, while using a slight interrogatory tone"), the Boomerang, the Columbo ("ask just part of a question, and trail off"); "When in doubt about whether to talk to the usability test participant, count to 10 silently." [7] Silence: "I count to seven before speaking… It is in periods of silence where participants often offer crucial and most-poignant information." [9]
- Leading questions: bad — "I saw you were having difficulty with the navigation. What happened?"; good — "What was easy or difficult about getting to the content you wanted?" [8] Convert closed to open: "Was it stressful?" → "How did you feel about that?" [18]
- 5 users: "After the fifth user, you are wasting your time by observing the same findings repeatedly"; "3 studies with 5 users each" beat one big study [6].
- Retrospective think-aloud (not NN/g): video-cued RTA gives "valid verbalizations… low risk of fabrication" (Guan 2006 via [14]); "at least doubles the time" [13]; concurrent verbalization "had a negative effect on task performance" [15] — relevant to a reflex game.

## 2. Techniques for people without the vocabulary

- **Word list (Gow et al. 2010)** [22]: Confused, Excited, Creative, Surprised, Satisfied, Powerful, Challenged, Confident, Bored, Determined, In Control, Curious, Immersed, Disappointed, Tense, Interested, Relaxed, Annoyed, Frustrated, Relieved — plus negations/opposites. "Word lists have the advantage that their role is constant throughout, whereas question prompting can vary considerably." Players were told "it was acceptable not to remember something, and not to guess."
- **Reaction cards (Benedek & Miner)** [20][21]: 118 words, 60% positive / 40% negative-neutral; pick five; interview on the picks.
- **Critical incident**: "Think back to a specific time when using [product] was [very frustrating]. Can you walk me through exactly what happened?" [24]
- **Laddering** ("why is that important to you?" up the chain): tedious past two or three rungs — "a bit like a three year old"; **negative framing is easier** to articulate than positive [25].
- **Expectation gap**: "How did you expect this to work?", "What did you try?", "Was there anything you found difficult?" [30]
- **Experience sampling**: Halo prompted every ~3 minutes with randomness; prompts right after combat inflated scores [43]; Volden et al. paused every minute with five affect chips and found confusion isn't always bad [29].
- **Comparison**: forced-choice between two segments on fun/challenge/frustration — coarse, needs both played [22].
- Thin: sentence completion, draw-the-experience, "bad first" — no verified playtest source; emotion wheels come from therapy, not UX.

## 3. Games practice

- Valve: "Nothing is quite so humbling as being forced to watch in silence as some poor playtester stumbles around your level for 20 minutes" (Birdwell); "you'll really tell by their body language whether or not they actually enjoyed themselves" (Swift) [32].
- Hodent: players said characters were too small to shoot; testing showed "the players actually didn't have problems shooting at the enemies" — say/do gap [35]; developers shouldn't run their own tests [36].
- Bromley: ask in "natural down-times"; "If you ask them 'Why did you not upgrade your horse armor', you are telling players that horse armor exists…" [38]; "This game is too hard isn't enough detail for a team to action." [39]
- Hopson's stack: pause-on-prompt UI, ~5 options with like/dislike always present, timestamps synced to video, "a perfect highlight reel" for designers who "hate reading reports or watching full playtest videos" [43].

## 4. Synthesis — what SPEEDHELL's pause-and-talk mode should do

1. One always-visible flag key that **pauses** and logs `{frame, stageT, section, lives, chain, enemies, bullets, recent events}` automatically — the "object" of the feeling for free.
2. Raw feeling first, in the player's words: "What were you doing here? What did you feel here?" [22] / "What's going through your mind?" [3]. Dictation counts.
3. Then the word list as an optional scaffold (Gow's 20 + SPEEDHELL's incoherence tags), followed by "tell me more about [word]".
4. Then the expectation gap: "What did you expect to happen? What did you try?"
5. Ladder once, negatively framed: "What about it made it feel [word]?" / "Compared to the wave before…"
6. The AI never asks "why", never names a system first, never asks for a fix during the interview — capture, then design later.
7. "I don't know" is a valid answer and still a data point (clusters of unexplained flags at one section are a finding).
8. Short sessions (per section beats full runs), debrief within 15 minutes.
9. Optional interval prompt, off by default, never right after a kill.
10. Pair every flag with a **replay cue** — a deterministic re-simulation to the flagged frame is a stronger cue than memory and has "low risk of fabrication".
11. Code each note on Gow's seven dimensions plus an object code, so notes aggregate across runs.
12. Jacob's flags = valid for feel/pacing, suspect for readability → second player for "what killed me" flags.

## Contradictions / thin evidence

Concurrent vs retrospective is a live debate (Nielsen wants brain-dump; RTA researchers show equal problem discovery with better coherence and no performance hit) — pause-and-talk is a hybrid neither camp studied. "Why" is endorsed by NN/g as a probe and warned against by game moderators; default to what/how. Reaction cards lack validity evidence. Miyamoto's over-the-shoulder habit is secondhand. No source gives a method for a developer interviewing himself.
